You lift a couple of times a week, maybe run or swim on the other days, and you keep hearing wildly different protein numbers. One friend says "a gram per pound." A label says 50 g a day. So how much do you actually need?

## The short answer, with honest hedges

For healthy adults who train regularly, the best available evidence suggests that eating around 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day covers most of the benefit for muscle and recovery. For an 80 kg person, that is roughly 100 to 130 g a day. Some people, especially those lifting seriously or eating in an energy deficit, may benefit from going a bit higher, toward 2.0 g/kg.

That is a range, not a prescription. It comes from studies in specific groups of people, and the upper end is less certain than the lower end. Let's look at where the numbers come from.

## What the biggest analysis found

The most cited piece of evidence here is a 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis by Morton and colleagues in the *British Journal of Sports Medicine* [1]. It pooled 49 randomized controlled trials with 1,863 healthy adults who did resistance training for at least six weeks, with or without extra protein.

Adding protein on top of training produced a real but modest benefit. On average, people gained about 0.3 kg more fat-free mass and about 2.5 kg more on their one-repetition maximum strength than people who trained without the extra protein [1]. Training itself did most of the work. Protein nudged the results.

The most useful finding for everyday readers was the dose. Using a statistical breakpoint analysis, the authors found that gains in fat-free mass plateaued at a total daily protein intake of about 1.6 g/kg [1]. Beyond that, extra protein did not add measurable muscle gains on average.

Here is the part that often gets dropped: the 95% confidence interval around that 1.6 g/kg figure ran from about 1.0 to 2.2 g/kg [1]. The authors themselves suggested that aiming a little higher, up to about 2.2 g/kg, may be reasonable for people who want to make sure they are covered. So "1.6" is a best estimate of where the average plateau sits, not a hard ceiling that applies to you personally.

## What the position stands recommend

Two major expert documents land in a similar place, which is reassuring because they were written by different groups using overlapping but not identical evidence.

The 2016 joint position statement from the American College of Sports Medicine, the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, and Dietitians of Canada recommends 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day for athletes, with intakes toward the higher end sometimes useful during intense training blocks or when eating less energy than you burn [2]. It also suggests spreading protein across meals every 3 to 5 hours and including some after key sessions.

The International Society of Sports Nutrition's 2017 position stand on protein and exercise recommends 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg/day for most exercising people who want to build and maintain muscle [3]. It adds that doses of roughly 20 to 40 g per meal (or about 0.25 g/kg) appear to maximally stimulate muscle protein synthesis in many people, ideally spread every 3 to 4 hours.

A note on context. These documents were written with athletes and dedicated exercisers in mind. If you train two or three times a week for general fitness, the lower half of these ranges is probably a sensible starting point. For reference, the standard recommended intake for adults in general is 0.8 g/kg/day, a figure designed to prevent deficiency in the general population rather than to support training.

## Does it matter how you spread it across the day?

Probably a little. The evidence here is more mechanistic than the daily total evidence, so it deserves a lower confidence rating.

The best known study on distribution is a 2013 trial by Areta and colleagues in *The Journal of Physiology* [4]. Twenty-four trained young men did a resistance exercise session and then drank 80 g of whey protein over the following 12 hours in one of three patterns: eight 10 g doses every 90 minutes, four 20 g doses every 3 hours, or two 40 g doses every 6 hours. The 20 g every 3 hours pattern produced the highest rates of myofibrillar protein synthesis, meaning the building of new contractile muscle proteins [4].

That is a useful clue, but it has real limits. It measured a short-term lab marker over 12 hours, not muscle growth over months. It used whey shakes, not mixed meals. And it included only young, trained men.

A 2018 review by Schoenfeld and Aragon in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* took on the popular claim that the body can only "use" about 20 to 25 g of protein at a time [5]. Their conclusion was that this idea is too simple. Larger doses do lead to more amino acids being burned for energy, but a meaningful share still goes toward building tissue, especially when protein comes in slower digesting whole foods. Based on the available data, they suggested that people aiming to maximize muscle growth could consume about 0.4 g/kg per meal across at least four meals, reaching at least 1.6 g/kg/day [5].

So the per-meal "limit" is less of a wall and more of a gentle curve. If you eat a big protein dinner, that protein is not wasted.

## What changes as you get older

This is where the evidence points to a real shift. Older muscle appears to respond less to the same dose of protein, a pattern researchers call anabolic resistance.

In a 2015 analysis in the *Journals of Gerontology*, Moore and colleagues pooled data from their lab's studies on how muscle protein synthesis responds to different protein doses at rest [6]. In healthy older men (average age about 71), the response plateaued at about 0.40 g/kg per meal. In healthy younger men (about 22), it plateaued at about 0.24 g/kg [6]. For a 75 kg person, that is roughly 30 g versus 18 g in a single meal.

Morton's meta-analysis pointed in the same direction from a different angle: the added benefit of protein supplementation on muscle gains got smaller with age [1]. That does not mean protein matters less for older adults. One reasonable interpretation is that older adults may need larger servings to get the same effect, and that resistance training becomes even more important alongside it.

Two cautions. The Moore data came from men only, and from acute lab measurements rather than long trials. Anyone with kidney disease or another medical condition should get protein guidance from their own clinician, since needs can differ.

## Where the uncertainty still lives

It is worth being clear about what we do not know well.

- **Women are underrepresented.** Much of the dose and distribution work was done in young men. The daily ranges likely apply broadly, but the per-meal numbers are less certain for women.
- **Short-term markers are not long-term results.** Muscle protein synthesis over a few hours is a useful signal, but it does not always predict muscle gained over months.
- **Body weight is a rough denominator.** Grams per kilogram of total body weight can overestimate needs for people with higher body fat. Some researchers prefer lean mass or a target body weight, though practical tools for this are limited.
- **Training type matters.** Most of the meta-analytic evidence comes from resistance training. Endurance athletes likely benefit from protein too, but the dose response data is thinner.
- **Energy intake changes the picture.** When people eat less than they burn, higher protein intakes appear more useful for keeping muscle, which is part of why position stands give a wide range [2].

**What the evidence shows:** In healthy adults doing resistance training, total daily protein up to about 1.6 g/kg improves gains in muscle mass on average, with the uncertainty band stretching to about 2.2 g/kg [1]. Position stands recommend 1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day for active people [2] [3].

**Our interpretation:** For most people training a few times a week, getting your daily total into roughly the 1.2 to 1.6 g/kg range matters far more than timing. Spreading protein across three or four meals is a sensible, low effort way to get there, and older adults may benefit from making each of those servings a bit larger.

**In practice (example):** One way a 70 kg person aiming for about 110 g could get there: Greek yogurt with oats at breakfast (about 25 g), a lentil and chicken salad at lunch (about 30 g), a glass of milk and a handful of nuts in the afternoon (about 15 g), and a salmon, rice, and vegetable dinner (about 35 g). This is an illustration, not a meal plan.

## What this could look like in a normal week

A few examples of how people might apply this, without turning it into a rule.

- **If you currently eat most of your protein at dinner,** one small change could be adding a protein source at breakfast, like eggs, yogurt, tofu, or cottage cheese.
- **If you train in the evening,** a regular dinner after your session likely covers the "post workout" window for most recreational exercisers. The position stands mention eating protein after key sessions [2], but there is no need to rush a shake in the locker room.
- **If you are over 60,** one option is to make sure each main meal includes a generous protein portion, closer to 30 g than 15 g, alongside regular strength work [6].
- **If you eat mostly plant foods,** combining sources like legumes, soy, grains, and nuts across the day can get you to the same totals.

In a sport where two sessions a day are normal, protein was never the hard part. Eating enough overall, and consistently, was. That lesson travels well to recreational training too.

## The takeaway

If you train a few times a week, aiming for roughly 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight per day is well supported, and going somewhat higher is reasonable but offers smaller and less certain returns. Spreading it across meals probably helps a little, and older adults likely need bigger servings to get the same response. Training consistently still does the heavy lifting.
