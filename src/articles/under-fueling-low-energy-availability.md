You train most days, sometimes twice. You eat foods you would describe as healthy. And yet you feel flat in sessions, cold when others are comfortable, and your progress has stalled. Could you be under fueling?

The short answer: possibly, and it is worth taking seriously. But those same feelings have many possible causes, and figuring out which one applies to you is a job for a clinician, ideally working with a sports dietitian. What we can do here is explain the idea behind under fueling, what the current consensus says, and why a diet that looks healthy can still fall short for someone with a heavy training load.

## The idea at the center: energy left over for the rest of you

Sports scientists use the term **energy availability** for the energy your body has left to run everything else after the cost of exercise has been paid [2]. Think of your food as a budget. Training takes its share first. What remains has to cover the quiet, essential work: making hormones, repairing tissue, building bone, fighting infections, keeping you warm, and supporting your mood and focus.

When that remaining share is too small for long enough, the body does what bodies do under scarcity. It starts to economize, turning down systems it can temporarily live without. That state is called **low energy availability**, and research across both women and men links it to changes in hormones, reproductive function, bone metabolism, and muscle [2].

You may see energy availability written as an equation in research papers. We are deliberately not turning it into a number for you to calculate at home, and the research itself explains why. Estimating it outside a lab is slow, expensive, and prone to errors that stack on top of each other, there is no universal protocol for how to calculate it, and what counts as "low" seems to vary between individuals [3]. A study of elite distance runners and race walkers found that energy availability was genuinely difficult to assess from food and training records, and that direct markers such as reproductive hormone status were at least as informative and more objective [4]. The 2023 IOC consensus also cautions against setting a single definitive clinical threshold, because so many factors moderate the effects [1].

## What the 2023 IOC consensus says about REDs

In 2023, the International Olympic Committee published an updated consensus statement on **Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport**, usually shortened to REDs [1]. It defines REDs as a syndrome of impaired physiological and/or psychological functioning, experienced by female and male athletes, caused by exposure to problematic (prolonged and/or severe) low energy availability [1].

A few points from that statement are especially useful for everyday athletes.

**Not all low energy availability is a problem.** The consensus describes a spectrum. Brief, mild dips can be adaptable, with small, quickly reversible changes and little or no long term impact. Problematic low energy availability is more prolonged or severe and can cause persistent disruption across body systems, often with noticeable signs or symptoms [1]. One hard week is not the same as months of running short.

**It affects many systems.** The statement lists potential effects on reproductive function, bone health, gut function, energy metabolism, blood health, mental health, thinking and concentration, heart and blood vessel function, and immunity, along with performance outcomes such as reduced training response, slower recovery, and lower strength [1].

**It is not only a women's issue.** The consensus is explicit that problematic low energy availability can occur in male athletes too, where some of the earliest signs can be harder to notice [1].

**It often happens unintentionally.** The statement notes that very high training volumes can lead to problematic low energy availability [1], which means an athlete can end up there simply because their eating has not kept pace with their training.

## Signs worth noticing, and why they are not a diagnosis

With all of that in mind, some signs that can be associated with under fueling include:

- Ongoing fatigue that rest days do not seem to fix
- Getting sick more often, or picking up injuries (especially bone injuries) that keep coming back
- Changes to the menstrual cycle, such as periods becoming irregular or stopping
- Performance that stalls or slides despite consistent training
- Feeling cold, low in mood, or struggling to concentrate

Two human studies illustrate why these matter. In a group of 10 junior elite female swimmers followed over a 12 week season, those whose ovarian hormones were suppressed showed markers of energy conservation and lower energy intake, and their 400 m swim performance declined, while swimmers with regular cycles improved [5]. The group was small and the design was observational, so it cannot prove cause, but in a sport where two-a-day sessions are normal it is a striking result. In elite distance athletes, those with menstrual disruption (in women) or low testosterone (in men) had several times more training days lost to bone injuries than their peers [4].

Here is the important part: **every one of these signs has other possible explanations.** Fatigue can come from poor sleep, overreaching, iron deficiency, thyroid problems, illness, or life stress. Menstrual changes have a long list of causes. Recurring illness can reflect many things. The IOC consensus says directly that each sign or symptom can arise from causes other than low energy availability, and that those other causes need to be ruled out when diagnosing REDs [1].

> A list of symptoms can tell you it is time to ask for help, but it cannot tell you what is going on.

So please do not use the list above as a self-diagnosis checklist. A list of symptoms can tell you it is time to ask for help, but it cannot tell you what is going on.

## Why "eating healthy" can still mean eating too little

This is the part that surprises a lot of committed, health minded athletes.

Many foods commonly praised as healthy, such as vegetables, salads, fruit, whole grains, and legumes, are high in fiber and water and relatively low in energy for their volume. They are filling. That is great for many people, but for someone with a very high training load, a plate built mostly on those foods can leave you feeling full long before you have eaten enough to cover training and everything else.

There is human evidence for this pattern. In a cross-sectional study of 25 female endurance athletes, those with menstrual disruption linked to low energy availability ate diets with lower energy density, less fat, and more fiber than athletes with regular cycles [6]. The authors suggested that a low energy density, high fiber pattern may be one practical contributor worth looking at [6]. It was a small study in one population (female endurance athletes), so treat it as a clue, not a rule for everyone.

Other everyday factors can add to the gap: busy schedules that squeeze out meals, training that blunts appetite for a while afterward, early sessions before breakfast, or simply not realizing how much more a big training block asks of you. None of this requires any intention to eat less. That is part of why under fueling can go unnoticed for so long.

**What the evidence shows:** The 2023 IOC consensus describes REDs as a syndrome caused by problematic low energy availability, affecting both female and male athletes across many body systems and performance [1]. Human studies link signs of low energy availability with worse performance and more bone injuries [4, 5], and one small study associates menstrual disruption with lower energy density, higher fiber diets in endurance athletes [6].

**Our interpretation:** For people who train a lot, "healthy" and "enough" are two separate questions. A diet can score well on the first and still fall short on the second, and that is a fueling mismatch, not a personal failing.

**In practice (example):** One way this could look is an athlete noticing months of fatigue, frequent colds, and stalled times, and booking an appointment with a doctor who works with athletes, bringing notes on training volume, typical meals, sleep, and any cycle changes. This is an illustration of asking for help, not a plan.

## Assessment belongs with clinicians and sports dietitians

Alongside the consensus, the IOC released a clinical tool, the REDs Clinical Assessment Tool, version 2 (REDs CAT2). It is designed for a physician with expertise in REDs to use, drawing on information from a wider team such as a sports medicine doctor, a sports dietitian, and a sports physiologist [1]. It combines multiple indicators rather than relying on any single number, and it includes ruling out other causes [1].

That is a good model to follow. If the description in this article sounds familiar, a sensible next step is to talk to your doctor, a sports medicine physician, or a registered dietitian who specializes in sport. They can look at the whole picture, run appropriate tests, and help you build a fueling approach that fits your training. We are not able to do that from an article, and we are not qualified to do it for you individually.

## The takeaway

Under fueling is real, common enough to have its own international consensus, and it can happen to athletes who eat carefully and train with commitment. The signs are worth noticing but are not specific, so they point toward a conversation with a qualified professional rather than a conclusion. And if you train a lot, remember that a diet can be healthy and still not be enough.
