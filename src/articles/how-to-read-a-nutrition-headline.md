A headline says coffee protects your liver, eggs raise your heart risk, or red meat causes cancer. Next month another one says the opposite. How do you know whether to change anything?

The short answer: most single nutrition studies that make the news are not strong enough, on their own, to justify changing what you eat. That does not mean nutrition science is useless. It means something more modest. A headline is the start of a question, not the answer. A handful of checks, most of which take seconds, will sort the signal from the noise.

## Why food seems linked to everything

In 2013, Jonathan Schoenfeld and John Ioannidis ran a clever experiment. They picked 50 common ingredients at random from recipes in a cookbook and searched the medical literature for studies linking each one to cancer [1].

Forty of the 50 ingredients (80%) had at least one study on cancer risk [1]. Among 264 single study estimates, 72% concluded that the food either raised or lowered risk [1]. But 75% of those estimates had weak statistical support or none at all [1].

Then came the telling part. When the authors looked at the 36 meta-analyses available for these ingredients (studies that pool many results together), only 26% reported an increased or decreased risk, and the average effect was essentially null [1]. Individual studies often reported implausibly large effects that shrank when the wider evidence was combined [1].

> A headline is the start of a question, not the answer.

This is the pattern to keep in mind. One study finding a dramatic link is common. A consistent effect across many well designed studies is much rarer, and much more worth your attention.

## Observational or randomized: the first thing to check

Most nutrition headlines come from **observational studies**. Researchers track what large groups of people eat and what happens to their health. These studies are valuable, especially for outcomes that take decades to develop, but they show associations. People who drink more coffee or eat more red meat differ in many other ways, including income, smoking, sleep, and overall diet. Statistical adjustment helps, but cannot account for everything.

A **randomized controlled trial** (RCT) assigns people by chance to one approach or another, which balances those hidden differences between groups. RCTs are better at showing cause and effect, though in nutrition they are often short, small, or measure risk markers like cholesterol rather than disease itself.

The two can disagree, sometimes sharply. Observational research had linked high blood levels of beta carotene with lower lung cancer risk, though that link may simply have reflected eating more fruit and vegetables [2]. When the Alpha Tocopherol, Beta Carotene (ATBC) trial randomized 29,133 Finnish male smokers aged 50 to 69, those given beta carotene supplements actually had a higher incidence of lung cancer (relative risk 1.18) [2]. A second trial, CARET, also found increased lung cancer incidence and total mortality [2]. The association was real. The causal story people drew from it was not.

A useful habit: look for words like "linked to," "associated with," or "tied to." Those almost always signal observational data. If the headline says "causes" but the study is observational, the headline has gone beyond the evidence.

## Relative risk versus absolute risk: a worked example

This is where many headlines quietly mislead, even when every number is technically correct.

In 2015, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified processed meat as a cause of colorectal cancer. Its working group estimated that each 50 g portion of processed meat eaten daily raises colorectal cancer risk by about 18% [3][4].

An "18% higher risk" is a **relative risk**. It tells you how much risk changes compared with a starting point. It does not tell you the starting point.

For the **absolute risk**, you need the baseline. According to US SEER data for 2021 to 2023, about 3.9% of men and women will be diagnosed with colorectal cancer at some point in their lives [5].

Here is the arithmetic, simplified for illustration:

- Baseline lifetime risk: about 3.9 in 100 people.
- An 18% relative increase: 3.9 × 1.18 ≈ 4.6 in 100 people.
- Absolute difference: about 0.7 percentage points, or roughly 7 extra cases per 1,000 people over a lifetime.

This is a simplification. The 18% figure applies to eating that amount every day, and a population average baseline does not describe any one person's risk. But the shape of the lesson holds: "18% higher risk" and "about 7 more cases per 1,000 people" describe the same finding, and they feel very different.

Neither framing is dishonest by itself. Seven per 1,000 matters across a whole country, and it may matter to you. The point is that you need both numbers to decide. Also worth knowing: IARC's classification reflects how strong the evidence is that something can cause cancer, not how large the risk is [4]. That is why processed meat can sit in the same category as tobacco without being anywhere near as dangerous.

## Where the spin often starts

It is tempting to blame journalists. The evidence suggests the story is more complicated.

Sumner and colleagues analysed 462 press releases on health research from 20 leading UK universities, along with the published papers and 668 related news stories [6]. Compared with the underlying papers, 40% of press releases contained exaggerated advice, 33% contained exaggerated causal claims, and 36% contained exaggerated inference to humans from animal research [6].

Those exaggerations traveled. When a press release exaggerated, news stories were far more likely to do the same: 58% for advice, 81% for causal claims, and 86% for inference to humans [6]. When the press release did not exaggerate, those rates fell to 17%, 18%, and 10% [6]. This was itself an observational study, so it cannot prove that press releases cause news exaggeration, but the association is strong.

The takeaway for readers: if possible, look past the news story and even the press release to the study abstract. The authors' own conclusion is sometimes more cautious than the headline.

## Red flags: mice, cells, and who paid

**Animal and cell studies.** Research in mice, rats, or cells in a dish is important for understanding mechanisms, but it frequently fails to predict what happens in people. A systematic review in the *BMJ* compared animal studies and human clinical trials for six medical treatments and found that results agreed for only two of them [7]. That review looked at medical treatments rather than foods, but the lesson generalizes: a finding in mice is a reason for more research, not a reason to change your breakfast. If a headline does not mention that a study was done in animals or cells, check.

**Funding.** Industry funding does not automatically make a study wrong, and much useful research is industry supported. But it is worth noticing. In a 2007 analysis of 206 nutrition articles on soft drinks, juice, and milk, articles funded entirely by industry had about 7.6 times the odds of reaching conclusions favorable to the sponsor compared with those with no industry funding, though that estimate was imprecise (95% confidence interval 1.27 to 45.73) [8]. Among interventional studies, none of the fully industry funded ones reached an unfavorable conclusion, compared with 37% of those without industry funding [8]. Funding disclosures are usually listed near the end of the paper.

## Evidence, interpretation, practice

**What the evidence shows:** Single studies linking foods to disease often report large effects that shrink or vanish when evidence is pooled [1]. Observational associations can point in the wrong causal direction [2]. Press release exaggeration is common and closely tracks exaggeration in the news [6].

**Our interpretation:** The most reliable nutrition guidance tends to come from consistent findings across many studies and designs, not from any single paper. When a headline surprises you, the most likely explanation is that the study is early, small, observational, or oversold, not that everything you knew was wrong.

**In practice (example):** A hypothetical headline reads "Daily coffee slashes dementia risk by 30%." Before acting, you might check: Was it in humans? Observational or randomized? What is the baseline risk, and so what is the absolute change? Does the abstract say "associated with" while the headline says "slashes"?

## A 60 second checklist

Next time a food headline catches your eye, run through these:

1. **Humans or not?** If mice or cells, file it under "interesting, not actionable."
2. **Observational or randomized?** "Linked to" and "associated with" usually mean observational.
3. **Relative or absolute?** If you only see a percentage change, ask: a percentage of what?
4. **One study or many?** A meta-analysis or a consistent body of research beats a single surprising result.
5. **Does the headline match the abstract?** Look for "causes" in the headline and "associated" in the paper.
6. **Who paid?** Note the funding, then weigh it alongside everything else.
7. **Who was studied?** Results from older male smokers or elite athletes may not apply to you.

## The takeaway

Most nutrition headlines describe one study, often observational, often framed in relative terms, and often amplified somewhere between the lab and your feed. You do not need to ignore them. Just ask a few questions before you let one change your plate.
