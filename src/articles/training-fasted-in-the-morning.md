Your alarm goes off, you have forty minutes before you need to be out the door, and breakfast feels like one step too many. Is heading out for a run or a spin class on an empty stomach a smart move, a mistake, or neither?

## The short answer

For most healthy people doing a session of under an hour, it probably does not matter much. Training fasted shifts your body toward burning more fat during the workout, but studies so far have not shown that this leads to more fat loss over weeks when total food intake is similar. For longer sessions, eating something beforehand tends to help performance a little.

There are some interesting differences in how muscle adapts to fasted training, but they are mostly measured in the lab and have not clearly translated into better race results. Here is the detail.

## What happens in your body during a fasted session

"Fasted" in these studies usually means after an overnight fast of about 8 to 12 hours, so a typical before breakfast workout.

A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis by Vieira and colleagues in the *British Journal of Nutrition* pooled 27 studies with 273 adults [1]. Aerobic exercise done fasted led to higher fat oxidation during the session than the same exercise done after eating. The difference was about 3 g of fat on average, and it showed up at low to moderate intensities (below about 70% of maximal aerobic capacity), not at higher intensities [1].

Three grams of fat is roughly 27 kcal. That is a real physiological difference, but a small one in the context of a whole day. The authors were also clear that their analysis looked only at acute effects during single sessions, and that it could not answer whether fasted training causes more fat loss over time [1].

A broader 2018 meta-analysis by Aird, Davies, and Carson in the *Scandinavian Journal of Medicine and Science in Sports* reached a consistent picture [2]. It included 46 studies and found that fasted exercise raised circulating free fatty acids (fats released into the blood for fuel), while eating beforehand raised insulin, as you would expect [2].

## Burning more fat in the session is not the same as losing more fat

This is the point that gets lost in a lot of fitness advice.

Your body balances fuel use across the whole day. If you burn a bit more fat during a morning session, you may burn a bit less later. What matters for body composition over time is the bigger picture of energy intake and expenditure across days and weeks.

The most direct test of this is a small randomized trial by Schoenfeld and colleagues, published in 2014 in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* [3]. Twenty young women followed the same reduced energy diet for four weeks and did one hour of steady aerobic exercise three times a week. Half trained after an overnight fast, and half had a meal replacement shake beforehand. Both groups lost weight and fat mass, with no meaningful difference between them [3].

> Your body balances fuel use across the whole day.

That study was short and small, and it only included young women, so it cannot settle the question for everyone. But it fits the broader picture: fasted training is not a shortcut to fat loss.

## Performance: when eating first helps

If your goal is to perform well in the session itself, timing starts to matter more as sessions get longer.

In the Aird meta-analysis, eating before exercise improved performance in aerobic sessions lasting longer than 60 minutes, with a small effect size. For sessions shorter than 60 minutes, there was no clear difference between fasted and fed [2]. Evidence for high intensity interval and sprint type work was too limited to draw firm conclusions, with only a handful of studies [2].

This matches the practical guidance in the 2016 joint position statement from the American College of Sports Medicine, the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, and Dietitians of Canada. It notes that eating carbohydrate in the hours before exercise can support endurance performance, particularly in longer events [4].

## Does fasted training change how muscles adapt?

This is where "train low" comes in: the idea of deliberately doing some sessions with low carbohydrate availability to amplify certain training adaptations.

The human evidence here is real but mixed, and it depends a lot on who is being studied.

In a 2011 trial by Van Proeyen and colleagues in the *Journal of Applied Physiology*, 20 physically active young men did six weeks of cycling training, four sessions a week, either fasted or with carbohydrate before and during sessions [5]. Both groups improved VO2max and 60 minute time trial performance by similar amounts. The fasted group showed larger increases in some muscle markers linked to fat metabolism, including enzyme activity and the breakdown of fat stored inside muscle, but these did not come with a performance advantage [5].

In a 2020 trial by Edinburgh and colleagues in the *Journal of Clinical Endocrinology and Metabolism*, men with overweight or obesity did six weeks of moderate intensity cycling either before or after consuming carbohydrate [6]. Those who trained before eating showed improved insulin sensitivity measures and greater use of fat stored in muscle, compared with those who trained after eating. Body mass changes did not differ between the groups [6]. This is a promising finding for metabolic health, but it is a single trial in one population, and it was not about athletic performance.

In trained athletes, the picture looks less exciting. A 2017 randomized trial by Gejl and colleagues in *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise* studied 26 elite male endurance athletes over four weeks [7]. Adding periodic carbohydrate restriction to their training gave no extra benefit to performance or muscle adaptations compared with normal carbohydrate intake [7].

So, fasted training reliably changes some metabolic markers. Whether that translates into better performance, especially for already well trained people, is still uncertain.

**What the evidence shows:** Fasted aerobic exercise increases fat burned during the session, mostly at low to moderate intensity [1] [2]. In the trial evidence we have, it has not produced greater fat loss than fed training when diet is matched [3]. Eating beforehand modestly helps performance in sessions longer than about an hour [2].

**Our interpretation:** For recreational exercisers doing easy or moderate sessions under an hour, training before breakfast is mostly a matter of preference, schedule, and how you feel. For longer or harder sessions, or when performance matters, eating something first is the safer choice. "Train low" strategies are better thought of as a tool some coached endurance athletes use occasionally than as a default.

**In practice (example):** One way someone might approach a week: an easy 30 minute morning jog done before breakfast because it fits the schedule, and a small snack like a banana or a slice of toast with honey before a longer weekend ride or a hard interval session.

## Who might want to skip fasted training

Fasted training is not a good fit for everyone. Keeping this general and educational:

- **People who feel lightheaded, shaky, nauseous, or unusually weak** when exercising without eating. That is useful feedback, and there is no evidence based reason to push through it.
- **People with diabetes or other conditions affecting blood sugar,** especially anyone who uses glucose lowering medication. Exercise timing and food interact with these treatments, so this is a conversation to have with your own healthcare team.
- **People who are pregnant, adolescents, and anyone with a history of disordered eating.** For these groups, skipping meals around training is generally not something to experiment with on your own, and guidance from a qualified professional matters.
- **People training a lot and eating too little overall.** When energy intake is chronically low relative to training, adding fasted sessions can make that gap harder to close.
- **Anyone preparing for a long or high intensity session** where performance matters, based on the performance evidence above [2].

## What carbohydrate before training could look like

You do not need a full breakfast to get most of the benefit. The ACSM joint position statement describes eating 1 to 4 g of carbohydrate per kg of body weight in the 1 to 4 hours before exercise for endurance events, adjusted to the person and the timing [4]. That upper end is for serious endurance efforts with time to digest. Before a normal morning session, something much smaller is common.

A few examples of what people eat when they have limited time:

- **About 15 to 30 minutes before:** a banana, a small handful of dried fruit, or a glass of juice. Quick to digest and light on the stomach.
- **About 60 minutes before:** toast with jam or honey, a small bowl of cereal with milk, or a rice cake with nut butter.
- **About 2 to 3 hours before (longer sessions):** porridge with fruit, a bagel with a little peanut butter, or yogurt with granola.

Lower fat and lower fiber options tend to sit better right before exercise, but this is highly individual. In a sport with early morning pool sessions, many swimmers learn what their stomach tolerates through trial and error. Testing a new snack on an easy day, rather than before a race, is a reasonable approach.

## The takeaway

Training before breakfast is fine for many healthy people doing shorter, easier sessions, and it is not a fat loss shortcut. For longer or harder workouts, a small carbohydrate snack is likely to help you perform better. The adaptations from fasted training are interesting, but in trained athletes they have not clearly led to better performance.
